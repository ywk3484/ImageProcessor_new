Summary

A page mean averages about 30,000 holes, so what limits it are errors all of them share. The prime suspects are blur that changes between scans and brightness leaking in through the background and noise. Measure which one dominates first (Step 0), then fix only that.

  • 1 nm is 0.05 px at 21 nm per pixel.

  • 1 % more blur moves CD by about 0.5–1 nm at your size. No filter can undo that; only a blur gauge can.

  • 10 % dimmer to 10 % brighter moved half_area by +0.2 nm and foot_area by −0.6 nm in my simulation. A fixed light smoothing cut that 4–5×.

What to try first:

  1. Step 0 on existing data (D1–D8): one script, about five lines back, naming the top error in nm.

  2. A fair baseline (M6): same holes in all datasets, 3σ pooled over 50+ pages.

  3. Two cheap fixes: stack each page's holes and measure the stack once (M1), and a fixed light smoothing. Also check that the pitch is not within 0.01 px of a whole number (D6).

  4. If the flicker is row-shaped, as the ACTIS design suggests: divide out each row's brightness (A1). That also unlocks a blur-resistant CD level (M4).

  5. A blur gauge per page (M5), from large-feature edges or the lattice's fine detail.

Pitch is easier: fitting the whole lattice (M8) is so precise that the nm-per-pixel scale becomes the limit. AI denoisers come last: the published CD evidence is thin and mixed, and none of it covers contact holes, actinic images or TDI sensors. Nothing here is proven on ACTIS data yet; the numbers come from my simulation and published work.

How the ACTIS forms an image

The ACTIS lights the mask with an image of a flickering tin plasma and sums only a few pulses per point. Brightness therefore changes over time and across the field, and the TDI scan sorts those changes onto rows and columns.

What this means for preprocessing:

  • The 20 % flicker is probably not random per hole. Critical illumination plus few pulses predicts brightness that is smooth across the field and changes row by row along the scan. Step 0 (D2) tests this in minutes.

  • If it is row-shaped, it can be removed almost completely. Each image row holds about 200 holes, enough to measure that row's brightness precisely.

  • Blur changes between scans are likely. The focus servo is good but not identical scan to scan, and blur is what moves CD most at your size (next section).

Where the nanometres go

A page mean averages about 30,000 holes, so random per-hole noise shrinks about 170 times. What is left are errors that many holes share. Preprocessing must target those.

Think of a page as a grid: each row is one moment in time, each column one fixed spot on the sensor (the TDI scan makes this exact, see the section above). Every error belongs to one hole, one row, one column or the whole page.

In a TDI scan the page is built line by line as the mask moves, so each row is a moment and each column a sensor spot.

Three numbers to keep in mind:

  • Target: 1 nm is 0.05 px at 21 nm per pixel.

  • Blur: at your size (100 nm hole, 60–75 nm blur), a 1 % change in blur width moves an area or width CD by about 0.5–1 nm. My earlier simulation gave +1.6 % CD for 3 % more blur. At this ratio, a slightly bigger hole and a slightly blurrier image look almost the same; no filter can tell them apart, only an independent blur measurement can.

  • Brightness: a perfectly brightness-proof method ignores the 20 % flicker. What leaks through is the background estimate, and noise that grows when a page is dim.

The two prime suspects for the page mean are therefore blur changes between scans and brightness leaking in through the background. Sensor-column bias and lattice-to-pixel phase come next. Step 0 measures which one it is before anything is built.

Step 0: find the dominant error first

Eight checks on data you already have show which error dominates; the first three decide most of the plan. None needs new scans.

In D3 the holes' own blur width is a fair blur gauge, because the mask area is the same in all three datasets. Holes are matched by position after aligning each page pair. An alignment error above half a pitch (~5 px) would pair the wrong holes, so the brightness or CD pattern confirms the match.

I would build this as one script for the remote that prints about five short lines, one per decision, and saves the rest to a file. Each check is first proven on synthetic pages with only that cause planted.

Fixes for the light-source flicker

If Step 0 finds the flicker row-shaped, as the ACTIS design predicts, measure each row's brightness from its ~200 holes and divide it out. Every method then becomes flicker-proof, including the model-based ones. If the flicker is truly per hole, keep brightness-proof methods and remove the noise leak instead.

Why fix it at all, if most methods already ignore a hole's brightness? Those methods assume the whole hole is scaled evenly. Three things break that:

  • A brightness change between rows inside one hole (5 rows tall) bends its shape.

  • Methods with a smooth background map cannot follow each hole's flicker. In my earlier simulation the deblended gaussian CD leaked +5 nm per 10 % flicker this way.

  • Without a fair brightness reference, the blur-resistant CD level of the measurement section cannot be used.

Guard rails. A1 removes only fast, row-to-row changes. Slow trends over many rows stay, so the real CD map of the mask survives. A region of larger holes brightens rows and columns together, which a row-only fix leaves alone. The planted-cause tests in the validation section check both.

Fixes for the TDI sensor position

The scan averages everything along the sensor's height, so "which part of the sensor" reduces to one number per hole: its sensor column. Column errors repeat in every scan, so they hurt repeatability only when the mask lands on different columns between datasets. They always bias CD maps. Your data can measure them, because every column sees thousands of holes per dataset.

Two cautions from other fields. Correct positions by subtracting the row shifts from measured centres, not by resampling the image; resampling adds its own position bias (Ye et al. 2020). And if every dataset puts the same holes on the same columns, B1–B4 will not change the repeatability number, only the map accuracy.

Your two datasets. The repeat scans share coordinates, but a mask reload can still move holes by a few columns: fit the lattice phase per stripe in each dataset to get its column offset before ruling B1–B4 in or out. The 8-shift dataset is what B4 needs: each hole read at 8 sensor positions separates the column profile (gain, CD bias, centre bias) from the mask. Solve it there, apply it as a fixed B1 map to the repeat scans, and check it holds across days. The spread of one hole's CD over the 8 shifts gives the column error in nm directly. Caveat: profiles much longer than the shift steps are weakly constrained by shifts alone; take those from B1 on the large datasets.

Noise reduction: traditional and AI

For the page mean, noise matters mostly through a small bias that changes with brightness, and a fixed light smoothing removes most of it. Stronger and AI denoisers mainly help per-hole maps, and each needs a bias check first.

Why noise still matters after averaging. Several CD methods pick a peak or a minimum from noisy pixels. Noisy pixels make the brightest pixel too bright and the darkest too dark. A dim page is relatively noisier, so its CD comes out slightly different from a bright page of the same mask.

How big, at your sizes. I simulated 100 nm holes, 70 nm blur, 21 nm pixels, photon noise of 4 % of the hole peak, and 8-bit rounding. Going from a page 10 % dimmer to one 10 % brighter moved half_area by +0.2 nm and foot_area by −0.6 nm. With noise of 8 % of the peak, it was +0.65 and −1.6 nm. A fixed 0.6 px Gaussian smoothing cut both 4–5×. gaussian, tail_area and edge_area hardly moved (under 0.05 nm). Without noise, no method moved at all.

The published evidence for AI denoisers in CD metrology is thin and mixed. On SEM line patterns, Hitachi (2024) found Noise2Void gave no gain on a noisy set and a slope of 1.22 against the reference on another. Their supervised network copied a 10 % linewidth shrink from its training references. imec reports unchanged mean CD after denoising, without numbers. No study covers contact holes, actinic images or TDI sensors.

My order: fixed light smoothing first, then the mean-hole method. Try AI only if per-hole maps need more, and only after the noise pattern (Step 0, D8) is known. Plain Noise2Void assumes each pixel's noise is independent; TDI noise along rows breaks that, which is what StructN2V and COSDD handle.

Resolution improvement

Your holes are already finely sampled: the blur alone is 3–3.6 px wide (60–75 nm), so no method can recover detail the optics did not pass. What does help is combining 30,000 holes into one noise-free, finely sampled average hole per page, and keeping blur the same from page to page.

Super-resolved average holeStack every hole at its own sub-pixel position into a 4× finer, almost noise-free picture of the page's average hole. Where a blur gauge can come from. Three sources, from most to least general:

  1. Edges of large features (pads, lines) on non-hole pages scanned just before or after. A large feature's edge shows the blur alone, whatever the hole size.

  2. The holes themselves, for repeat scans only. The three datasets image the same mask, so any change in the holes' width between them is blur. This is what Step 0 (D3) uses.

  3. The lattice pattern's fine detail. Blur weakens the finer repeats of the lattice (its higher Fourier harmonics) faster than the coarse ones. The ratio is a blur gauge for the same mask, not across different CDs.

Measurement changes that turn cleaner images into better repeatability

Three changes do most of the work. Measure the page's average hole instead of averaging per-hole CDs. Choose a CD definition that resists blur. Keep the hole list and pixel phase from moving the page mean. All are new methods beside the existing ones; none replaces a method you have.

Validation: how to prove each step helps

Judge every step by one number, the dataset-to-dataset spread of page means pooled over many pages, plus two guards: the method must still follow real CD changes, and it must not add brightness dependence.

  1. The main number. For each page, take the spread of its three page means across datasets, then pool over at least 50 pages. One page with three values is a weak estimate: its 3σ can be off by a factor of 2 or more. Pooling 100 pages pins it to about ±10 %.

  2. Paired comparison. Same pages, same holes, with and without the step. Use only holes measured in all three datasets, so a changed hole list cannot move the mean.

  3. Slope guard. A smaller spread is better only if the method still follows a real CD change one-for-one. On synthetic pages with planted CD steps (for example a region 2 nm larger), the response must not drop. On real pages, the CD map's real pattern must keep its size and stay alike across datasets.

  4. Brightness guard. Plot page-mean CD against page brightness, and per-hole CD against per-hole brightness. The trend must flatten, never steepen.

  5. Planted-cause tests. Synthetic pages with one cause each: row flicker, column gain, 1 % more blur, a 10 % brighter page, a whole-number pitch. Each step must remove its own cause and add no other.

  6. Noise floor. Split each page's holes into two interleaved halves; the gap between their means shows the purely random part. Whatever the dataset-to-dataset spread holds beyond that is shared error, the real target.

  7. No per-page targets. Never scale each page or dataset to the same CD; that fakes repeatability. Calibrate once and reuse it for every dataset.

What not to try

These look attractive but either add bias, erase real signal, or fake the result.

  • Scaling each page or dataset to the same CD. It makes repeatability look perfect by construction.

  • Dividing a hole's CD by its cell's mean brightness. The cell mean grows with hole area, so this removes the CD signal together with the blur effect (research simulation).

  • Deconvolution before measuring. No new information, more noise, and with an unknown blur it brings back the blur–size confusion.

  • AI super-resolution (GAN, diffusion). The extra detail comes from training data, not from your mask.

  • Generic stripe filters (Fourier or wavelet destriping). They act at the same spatial frequencies as your 10 px lattice and erase real pattern. Fit row and column profiles instead.

  • Strong non-linear denoising without the brightness test. Total-variation smoothing loses contrast by feature size, not brightness, which biases small holes (Strong, Aujol & Chan). BM3D and non-local means with one global setting need the same test.

  • Plain Noise2Void on row-correlated noise. It assumes every pixel's noise is independent; use StructN2V or COSDD if D8 finds row structure.

  • Supervised denoisers trained on mismatched references. Hitachi's network copied a 10 % linewidth shrink from its references (Chen et al. 2024).

  • Merging the three datasets into one image. They are your only repeatability test; keep them separate.

  • Resampling the image to remove stage wobble. Subtract row shifts from the measured centres instead.

  • Brightness (flux) CD against a reference, disk fits, PSF or nominal-CD inputs. Flux CD and disk fits failed on your data; PSF and nominal-CD inputs you ruled out.

Roadmap

Phase 1 needs only the data you already have. Phase 2 fixes one error per pass, then re-runs Step 0, until the page means repeat. AI denoisers wait for phase 3.
